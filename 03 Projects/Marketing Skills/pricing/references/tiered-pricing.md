# Tiered Pricing — Good / Better / Best

Tiered pricing is the most common offer structure. Three tiers, named well, gives the buyer a frame for choosing and lifts total revenue vs. single-tier pricing. Get it wrong and you confuse buyers or look manipulative.

---

## When to use tiered pricing

Use tiered pricing when:
- The buyer has variable needs and budget.
- You have variable fulfillment cost across tiers.
- You want to anchor a mid-tier against a high-tier.
- Conversion on a single tier is stuck — tiers give buyers a frame.

Don't use tiered pricing when:
- The offer is genuinely one thing (a single product, one service).
- The fulfillment cost doesn't vary by tier.
- Buyers find tiers confusing (rare, but real in some markets).
- You don't have the operational capacity for multiple fulfillment tracks.

---

## The 3-tier pattern (Good / Better / Best)

### Structure

| Tier | Role | Typical price | Conversion share |
|------|------|---------------|------------------|
| **Good** (entry) | Accessible entry, often self-serve | ~30–40% of mid | 20–40% of buyers |
| **Better** (target) | Most fulfillment, best value, where you want them | ~50–60% of high | 50–70% of buyers |
| **Best** (premium) | 1:1, exclusive, high-touch | 2–3× the mid | 5–15% of buyers |

The middle tier is the target — most fulfillment capacity goes there, most marketing points there. The high tier is the decoy/anchor. The low tier is the accessible entry.

### Naming

Don't use the literal names "Good / Better / Best" on the offer page. Use names that describe what the buyer gets, not where they rank.

**Examples:**

| Generic | Better names |
|---------|--------------|
| Good / Better / Best | Self-paced / Cohort / 1:1 |
| Basic / Pro / Enterprise | Starter / Growth / Scale |
| Bronze / Silver / Gold | DIY / DFY / DWY (Do With You) |

**Naming rules:**
- Each name should communicate what's different.
- Avoid superlatives ("Ultimate", "Premium Plus") that don't differentiate.
- Match names to the buyer's mental model.

## Designing each tier

### Tier 1 (Good / entry)

**Purpose:** Accessible entry. Buyer who's not ready to commit fully. Or buyer who doesn't need the higher tiers.

**Common shape:**
- Self-paced or async
- Lower-touch support
- Core deliverable only, no bonuses
- Lower price point

**Common mistake:** making Tier 1 so stripped down that it's not viable. If Tier 1 doesn't deliver real value, the buyer regrets the purchase and never upgrades.

**Rule:** Tier 1 must deliver the core outcome, just less support. Not a stripped-down version that fails.

### Tier 2 (Better / target)

**Purpose:** Where you want most buyers. The right balance of support, deliverable, and price.

**Common shape:**
- Live cohort or group calls
- All core deliverables + bonuses
- Mid-touch support
- Mid price

**Common mistake:** making Tier 2 too similar to Tier 1 or Tier 3. If the gap to Tier 1 is small, buyers choose Tier 1. If the gap to Tier 3 is small, buyers choose Tier 3.

**Rule:** Tier 2 should be the obvious choice for the median buyer. The gap up to Tier 3 should feel like a stretch, the gap down to Tier 1 should feel like a compromise.

### Tier 3 (Best / premium)

**Purpose:** Premium option for buyers who want 1:1 or exclusive access. Also serves as anchor for Tier 2.

**Common shape:**
- 1:1 calls with operator
- Custom deliverables
- Direct access / priority support
- 2–3× Tier 2 price

**Common mistake:** making Tier 3 transparently bad as a decoy. Buyers see through this.

**Rule:** Tier 3 must be a real, valuable offer — not a stripped-down "Enterprise" tier with features no one wants. Some buyers will actually buy it. Those buyers need a real product.

## Pricing the tiers

The price ratios matter. Get them wrong and one tier looks obviously bad.

**Standard ratios:**

| Tier | Ratio to Tier 2 |
|------|----------------|
| Tier 1 | 0.3–0.5× Tier 2 |
| Tier 2 | 1.0 (baseline) |
| Tier 3 | 2.0–3.5× Tier 2 |

**Worked examples:**

| Tier 2 (mid) | Tier 1 | Tier 3 |
|--------------|--------|--------|
| $500 | $197 (0.4×) | $1,497 (3×) |
| $1,500 | $497 (0.33×) | $4,500 (3×) |
| $5,000 | $1,500 (0.3×) | $15,000 (3×) |

**Anti-pattern:** Tier 3 at the same price as Tier 2 with slightly more. Buyers pick Tier 2.

**Anti-pattern:** Tier 1 priced too close to Tier 2. Buyers can't justify paying 2× for what looks like a small upgrade.

---

## When to use 1 tier (not tiered)

Single-tier pricing is right when:
- The offer is genuinely one thing (an ebook, a single workshop).
- You're optimizing for simplicity (low-ticket info products).
- Your fulfillment capacity is fixed and tiering doesn't help.
- You're early-stage and don't have data to design tiers well.

Don't tier what doesn't need tiering. A $27 ebook with 3 tiers is confusing.

---

## When to use 2 tiers

Two-tier pricing works when:
- You want to test tiered structure without committing to three.
- The audience naturally splits into "self-serve" and "1:1" with no middle.
- You want to filter for high-commitment buyers (1:1 only, no group option).

**2-tier example:**

| Tier | Price |
|------|-------|
| Cohort | $2,000 |
| 1:1 VIP | $7,500 |

2-tier is clean. The buyer picks. No decoy, no manipulation.

---

## When to use 3 tiers

3-tier is the default for most paid offers because:
- It gives the decoy effect (most buyers pick middle).
- It serves different buyer types (self-serve, supported, premium).
- It anchors Tier 2 against Tier 3.
- It's the pattern buyers are trained on.

3-tier is right for most offers above $500.

---

## When to use 4+ tiers

Don't. Four tiers confuse buyers and dilute the decoy effect. If you have a legitimate fourth option, fold it into one of the existing tiers.

**Rare exception:** enterprise + custom. A 4th tier for "$25,000+, contact us" can work in B2B contexts. Keep it on a separate page.

---

## Tier design anti-patterns

### Tier 1 is transparently bad
"Self-paced only, no calls, no bonuses." Buyer picks Tier 2 by default. Tier 1 looks like theater.

### Tier 3 is transparently bad
"$5,000 for one 30-min call." The decoy is obvious. Buyers feel manipulated.

### Tier names are vague
"Basic / Plus / Premium" without communicating what's different.

### The middle tier has too many features
Tier 2 has 14 features; Tier 3 has 16. Buyer can't tell what's worth the upgrade.

### Tier price gaps are wrong
Tier 2 = $1,000, Tier 3 = $1,200. Gap is too small. Tier 3 wins on value, Tier 2 looks overpriced.

### Tiers compete with the same buyer
Tier 1 has 80% of the features of Tier 3 at 30% of the price. Tier 3 never sells. Make tiers serve different needs, not the same need at different price.

---

## Tier design checklist

- [ ] Each tier serves a real buyer type (not just a decoy)
- [ ] Names communicate what's different
- [ ] Tier 1 delivers the core outcome (just less support)
- [ ] Tier 2 is the obvious choice for the median buyer
- [ ] Tier 3 is a real premium offer some buyers will choose
- [ ] Price ratios are 0.3–0.5× / 1× / 2–3.5×
- [ ] No tier is transparently bad
- [ ] Each tier has different fulfillment, not just different price

## Worked example: course creator

**Single offer:** "The Course" at $497.

**Re-tier:**

| Tier | Name | What's included | Price |
|------|------|-----------------|-------|
| 1 | Self-paced | 6 modules, lifetime access | $197 |
| 2 | **Cohort** | 6 modules, 8 weekly calls, written plan by Day 14 | $997 |
| 3 | VIP | Everything in Tier 2 + 2x 1:1 calls with operator | $2,997 |

**Tier 1** is real — buyer gets the modules. Just no support.

**Tier 2** is the target. 70% of buyers end up here. The cohort lifts completion and outcome rates vs. self-paced.

**Tier 3** is real VIP. 10% of buyers pick it. The 1:1 calls are high-leverage for those who want them.

**Price ratios:** $197 / $997 / $2,997 = 0.2× / 1× / 3×. Tier 1 is on the low end (could push to $297), Tier 3 is on target.

**Anti-pattern check:**
- Tier 1 isn't transparently bad — it's a real product.
- Tier 3 isn't a decoy — some buyers actually want it.
- Tier 2 is the obvious median choice.
- Names communicate what's different (self-paced vs cohort vs VIP).
