# Anchor Analysis — 3 Anchor Types

The anchor is the reference price the buyer compares against. Without an anchor, any price reads as expensive. With a strong anchor, the right price reads as fair.

The discipline: find a real anchor, frame it specifically, surface it on the offer page.

---

## The 3 anchor types

### 1. Alternative Cost Anchor

**What:** The price of doing it some other way (DIY, agency, hire, status quo).

**Example:** "$5,000 — vs. $25,000 agency engagement for the same scope."

**When it works:** Services, agencies, coaching where the buyer has a known alternative. Strong when the alternative is expensive, slow, or risky.

**Strength:** Very strong when real. Buyers respect the comparison.

**Risk:** If the alternative cost is fake or exaggerated, the anchor collapses. Buyers can call agencies and check.

**Real-anchor checklist:**
- [ ] Can the buyer verify this alternative exists?
- [ ] Is the alternative cost reasonable (not exaggerated)?
- [ ] Is the comparison fair (same scope, not apples-to-oranges)?

**Framing pattern:**
> "[Your price] — vs. $[alternative cost] for [what they would otherwise do]."

---

### 2. Total Value Anchor

**What:** The sum of the deliverable's value (core + bonuses), against which the offer price is a fraction.

**Example:** "$497 — total value $2,397 (you're saving $1,900)."

**When it works:** Offers with strong, defensible bonus stacks. Info products and courses.

**Strength:** Weakest of the three. Inflation theater has trained buyers to distrust total-value claims.

**Risk:** Inflated values. If the bonus values don't survive scrutiny, the anchor collapses and the price reads as expensive.

**Real-anchor checklist:**
- [ ] Are bonus values real or honestly perceived?
- [ ] Could the buyer buy each bonus separately at the stated price?
- [ ] Is the math conservative (round numbers, not "was $2,397, now $497")?

**When to skip:** If you can't defend the bonus values honestly, don't use a total-value anchor. Use an alternative-cost anchor instead, or skip the anchor entirely and lean on payment structure.

**Framing pattern:**
> "Total value: $[X] · Your price: $[Y] · You save: $[X-Y]."

---

### 3. Future Cost of Inaction Anchor

**What:** What the buyer loses by NOT buying. The cost of the problem continuing.

**Example:** "$5,000 — vs. losing $40k MRR over the next 12 months if this stays broken."

**When it works:** High-ticket offers where the problem has measurable, ongoing cost. B2B consulting, premium coaching, agency engagements.

**Strength:** Strongest psychologically — buyers fear losses more than they value gains. But only works when the cost of inaction is real and specific.

**Risk:** If the cost of inaction is inflated, the anchor feels manipulative. The buyer senses this.

**Real-anchor checklist:**
- [ ] Is the cost of inaction measurable (not vague "missed opportunities")?
- [ ] Is the timeframe specific (not "eventually")?
- [ ] Is the cost something the buyer already feels (or can quickly grasp)?

**Framing pattern:**
> "[Your price] — vs. continuing to lose $[X] every [month/quarter] because [problem]."

---

## Picking the right anchor

| If your offer is... | Use this anchor | Backup |
|---------------------|-----------------|--------|
| Service / agency | Alternative cost | Future cost of inaction |
| Course / info product | Total value (if honest) | Alternative cost (vs. mentorship) |
| 1:1 coaching | Future cost of inaction | Alternative cost (vs. consulting) |
| High-ticket B2B | Future cost of inaction | Alternative cost (vs. hiring in-house) |
| Low-ticket info product | Skip anchor | Total value (be careful) |

**Default rule:** if you can find a real alternative-cost anchor, use it. It's the most defensible.

---

## Where to surface the anchor

The anchor should appear:
- [ ] On the sales page — within the first 30% of the page (above the offer section)
- [ ] In the offer section — right next to the price
- [ ] In launch emails — once or twice across the sequence
- [ ] In proposals — as the comparison in the investment section

Don't surface the anchor everywhere. It's a reference point, not a sales pitch. Three to five touches across a launch is enough.

---

## Anchoring anti-patterns

### Fake prior price

"Was $999, NOW $499" with no actual $999 history. Buyers can check. The FTC has rules about this for a reason.

### Inflated alternative cost

"vs. $50,000 for the same thing at [unnamed agency]." If you can't name the agency or the comparison is unfair, don't use it.

### Inflated total value

"Total value: $5,000, yours for $97" with bonus values that wouldn't survive a buyer's math.

### Multiple competing anchors

Three different anchor types on the same page. The buyer gets confused or distrustful.

### Anchor that's a downgrade

Anchoring your $5k offer against a $1k alternative makes your offer look bad, not good. Pick anchors that are higher than your price, not lower.

## Worked example

**Offer:** "The 90-Day MRR Sprint" — coaching for founders.

**Pricing brief:**
- Floor: $4,000 (operator time + 30% margin)
- Comparable: Founder coaching at $5k–$15k from peer operators
- Alternative: Hire a sales consultant for $20k–$50k
- Future cost: $30k MRR gap × 12 months = $360k lost revenue

**Anchor choice:** Alternative cost. "vs. $25,000+ for a sales consultant who takes 6 months to ramp."

**Final price:** $7,500 one-time or 3 × $2,750.

**Anchor framing:** "Most founders pay $25,000+ for a sales consultant who takes 6 months to ramp. We do this in 90 days for $7,500 — or 3 × $2,750."

The anchor is real, specific, and higher than the price. The price reads as fair against it.
