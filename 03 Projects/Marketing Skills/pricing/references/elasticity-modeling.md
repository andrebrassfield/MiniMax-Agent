# Elasticity Modeling — How Demand Changes With Price

Elasticity is how much demand changes when you change the price. Most operators guess wrong about this. The discipline: measure, then decide.

---

## The basic shape

For most offers, raising the price lowers conversion but increases revenue per buyer. Lowering the price raises conversion but decreases revenue per buyer.

```
Revenue = Price × Conversion × Traffic
```

The question is: which lever moves revenue most?

**Inelastic demand:** raising the price barely affects conversion → revenue goes up.
**Elastic demand:** raising the price kills conversion → revenue goes down.

Most offers are somewhere in between, and the elasticity changes with price level. The discipline is finding where your offer sits.

---

## How to think about elasticity

### High-elasticity offers (price-sensitive)

- Lots of alternatives (commodity-ish).
- Buyer is comparing multiple options.
- Low commitment to your specific solution.
- Price is the dominant decision factor.

**Implication:** Lower prices convert more, but revenue per buyer is low. Compete on volume or differentiate hard.

### Low-elasticity offers (price-insensitive)

- Few real alternatives (you have a unique mechanism or audience).
- Buyer is committed once they understand the offer.
- Other factors (trust, proof, fit) dominate the decision.
- Price is a secondary factor after fit.

**Implication:** Higher prices work — the buyer who wants this will pay it. Volume is lower per channel, but revenue per buyer is much higher.

### How to estimate which you're in

Ask: if I raised the price 30%, would I lose most of my buyers or most of them?

- Most → high elasticity → competitive differentiation needed.
- Few → low elasticity → raise the price, raise the floor.

---

## When to raise the price

Raise the price when:

1. **Conversion is strong (>3–5%)** but revenue per buyer feels low.
2. **The offer has gotten better** — more proof, more bonuses, more guarantees.
3. **You have capacity pressure** — more demand than you can serve.
4. **Your fulfillment cost has gone up** — operator time, support cost, etc.
5. **The market has matured** — buyers expect to pay more.

How much to raise: 20–50% in one move, then hold for 3+ months. Smaller raises don't move buyer behavior. Repeated small raises ("$10 increase") read as grasping.

## When to lower the price

Lower the price when:

1. **Conversion is very low (<1%)** at the current price.
2. **Buyers aren't engaging** — traffic is fine, but no one reaches the offer.
3. **Competitors are materially cheaper** with similar offers.
4. **The offer is genuinely lower-tier** — fewer deliverables, less support.

How much to lower: 30–50% in one move to test. Don't creep down 10% at a time — it kills anchoring and signals desperation.

## When to hold

Hold the price when:

1. **Conversion is healthy** (2–5% for warm traffic).
2. **You can't raise fulfillment cost** without breaking margin.
3. **You don't have enough data** to know if elasticity is favorable.

Holding is the underused lever. Most operators churn the price. A stable price lets you test other variables (offer, copy, traffic).

---

## Common mistakes

### Mistake: assuming elasticity is the same in both directions

Lowering the price doesn't always raise conversion. Sometimes it signals "this is low quality" and conversion goes down. Test, don't assume.

### Mistake: treating elasticity as fixed

Elasticity changes with:
- Audience quality (cold vs warm)
- Offer strength (with/without bonuses)
- Proof (with/without testimonials)
- Time of year (B2B is more elastic in Q4)
- Market conditions (recession makes elasticity higher)

### Mistake: optimizing for conversion instead of revenue

100% conversion at $1 = $1 per visitor. 5% conversion at $200 = $10 per visitor. Always check revenue per visitor, not just conversion.

### Mistake: never testing the price

If you haven't tested the price, you don't know your elasticity. You're guessing. Testing methodology below.

---

## When price testing makes sense

| Situation | Test the price? |
|-----------|-----------------|
| New offer, no history | No — pick a reasonable price, ship, learn |
| Existing offer, steady conversion | Yes — test a 30%+ price change |
| Conversion dropping | Maybe — first check offer/copy/traffic before testing price |
| Enterprise sales | No — sales-driven, not elasticity-driven |

Don't test the price when you don't have enough traffic to read results. You need at least 100 buyers per arm to draw conclusions. Below that, you're reading noise.

---

## Quick decision framework

```
Is conversion healthy (2-5%) and revenue per buyer feels low?
├── Yes → Raise price 20-50%, hold 3 months
└── No → Is conversion very low (<1%)?
    ├── Yes → Lower price 30-50%, test
    └── No → Offer/copy problem, not pricing — go back to /offers
```

This isn't a substitute for actual data. But it's the right starting heuristic when you don't have data.
