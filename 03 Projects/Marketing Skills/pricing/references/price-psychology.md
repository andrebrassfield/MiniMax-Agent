# Price Psychology

Price isn't just a number — it's a signal. Charm pricing, premium positioning, framing, and decoys all shape how the buyer reads the price. Use them deliberately.

---

## Charm pricing vs premium pricing

**Charm pricing** — prices ending in 9, 7, or 5. $97, $199, $997. Leverages the "left-digit effect" — buyers read $9.97 as smaller than $10.00.

**Premium pricing** — round numbers. $100, $1,000, $10,000. Signals confidence and quality. No left-digit trick.

### When to use charm pricing

- Low-to-mid ticket ($10–$500).
- Commodity or near-commodity offers where the price is the differentiator.
- High-volume e-commerce, info products, online courses.
- Buyer is comparing many options.

### When to use premium pricing

- Mid-to-high ticket ($500+).
- Premium positioning — boutique, exclusive, high-touch.
- Buyer is comparing few options.
- The buyer expects to pay a round number for serious work.

**Worked example:**

| Offer | Charm | Premium |
|-------|-------|---------|
| $97 info product | ✓ | |
| $497 cohort course | ✓ (but $500 also works) | ✓ |
| $2,500 coaching | | ✓ ($2,500 reads as confident) |
| $9,997 mastermind | | ✓ ($9,997 reads as hiding the round number) |
| $25,000 consulting | | ✓ ($25,000 only — never $24,997) |

**Rule of thumb:** charm pricing below $500, premium above. Between $500–$2,500 is judgment call — match the operator's positioning.

## The decoy effect

A decoy is a third option that exists to make one of the other two look better.

**Classic setup:** three tiers — Basic, Pro, Enterprise. Pro is the target. Enterprise is the decoy (priced so high that Pro looks like a steal).

**Example:**

| Tier | Price | What's included |
|------|-------|-----------------|
| Basic | $497 | Self-paced course |
| **Pro** | **$1,497** | **Course + group calls + bonuses (target)** |
| Enterprise | $4,997 | Course + 1:1 with founder |

Without Enterprise, Pro looks expensive. With Enterprise, Pro looks like a deal.

**Decoy design rules:**
1. The decoy must be **visibly worse** than the target on a key dimension.
2. The decoy must be **priced high enough** to make the target look like a deal.
3. The decoy must be **plausible** — buyers shouldn't think it's a joke.
4. Don't make the decoy so bad that buyers feel manipulated.

**Anti-pattern:** a decoy tier that everyone can see is bad. "Premium" tier with stripped-down features at the same price as Pro. Buyers feel tricked.

## Anchoring psychology (in copy)

Anchors work because buyers compare. The first number they see sets the reference.

**Pattern 1: Anchor above the price**

> "Most agencies charge $25,000 for this scope. We do it for $7,500."

The $25,000 sets the reference. $7,500 reads as a deal.

**Pattern 2: Anchor via comparison**

> "Same outcome as a $50,000 consulting engagement. At a fraction of the cost."

Comparison anchors are stronger than abstract anchors.

**Pattern 3: Anchor via loss**

> "Every month you wait costs you $40k in lost MRR. This program fixes it in 90 days for $7,500."

Loss framing makes the price feel small against the cost of inaction.

**Pattern 4: Multiple anchors**

> "Most coaching programs charge $15k+. Mine is $5,000. AND I include [bonus usually sold for $2k]. Total value $7,000+."

Stacked anchors can work if each is real.

## Framing the price

How the price is framed changes how it feels.

### Investment vs cost

**Cost framing:** "$7,500 to take this course."
**Investment framing:** "$7,500 invested in your MRR."

Investment framing works when the buyer expects ROI. Cost framing works for one-time purchases.

### Monthly vs total

**Total:** "$7,500 for the program."
**Monthly:** "$625/month for 12 months."

Monthly framing reduces the sticker shock. Total framing shows the real commitment.

**When to use each:**
- Total: when the buyer has the cash and wants to see total commitment.
- Monthly: when the buyer has cashflow concerns or is price-sensitive.

### Per-day vs lump-sum

**Lump-sum:** "$7,500 for the program."
**Per-day:** "$83/day for 90 days."

Per-day framing works for offers with fast time-to-result. The buyer thinks "less than my morning coffee."

### Outcome vs cost

**Cost:** "$7,500."
**Outcome:** "$7,500 to add $40k MRR."

Outcome framing anchors against the result, not the cost. Strong when the outcome is specific and measurable.

## Pricing and the buyer's mental model

Buyers don't optimize on price. They optimize on **perceived value** and **perceived risk**.

**Perceived value levers:**
- Specific outcome ("add $40k MRR").
- Proof of result (testimonials, case studies).
- Mechanism explained (how it works).
- Premium positioning (round numbers, exclusive feel).
- Anchor comparison (vs. alternative cost).

**Perceived risk levers:**
- Guarantee (the bigger, the better).
- Payment structure (multi-pay reduces cash risk).
- Refund policy (clear, visible).
- Social proof (others have done it).
- Operator credibility (track record).

When conversion is stuck on price, the issue is usually one of these two. Not the number.

## Cognitive biases that work for and against you

### For you

- **Anchoring** — first number sets the reference.
- **Loss aversion** — losses feel 2x as much as equivalent gains.
- **Decoy effect** — buyers pick the middle of three.
- **Charm pricing** — $97 < $100 psychologically.
- **Premium positioning** — higher price = higher quality (sometimes wrongly).

### Against you

- **Sticker shock** — round-number prices feel bigger than they are.
- **Comparison anchoring** — buyers compare to the cheapest option they've seen.
- **Sunk cost fallacy** — buyers who've paid for something cheap feel they should get something cheap.
- **Anchoring to competitors** — if competitors are cheaper, your price reads as expensive.

Use the "for you" biases deliberately. Mitigate the "against you" biases with anchoring, framing, and proof.

## What doesn't work

### "Was $999, NOW $499"
Fake prior prices. Buyers can check. FTC violation in some cases. Don't.

### Mystery pricing
"Contact us for pricing." Reads as expensive without an anchor. Always show the price with an anchor.

### "Starting at $X"
Starting at $99 is misleading if most buyers pay $1,000. Be specific.

### Hidden fees
Price on the page, fees at checkout. Conversion-killing. Show total price upfront.

### Tier obfuscation
Three tiers with confusing names. Make tier names clear and the differences obvious.

---

## Price psychology summary

1. **Choose charm or premium based on price level.** Below $500 = charm. Above $500 = premium.
2. **Always anchor the price.** Three types: alternative cost, total value, future cost of inaction.
3. **Frame the price with the buyer's mental model.** Investment, monthly, per-day, outcome.
4. **Use the decoy effect for tiered offers.** A bad third tier makes the middle tier look great.
5. **Don't fake prices, hide prices, or hide fees.** Anchors only work when they're real.
