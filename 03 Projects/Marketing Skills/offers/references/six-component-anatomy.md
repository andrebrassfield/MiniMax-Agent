# Six-Component Anatomy

Every offer is built from six components. Build each one explicitly. None of them is optional.

The list order matters: 1–3 are substance (what they get), 4–5 are presentation (why now, what it's called), 6 is the math (what it costs).

---

## 1. Core Deliverable

**Question:** What do they actually get?

### What makes a good core deliverable

- **Specific.** "Four 60-minute calls" beats "support calls". "Six modules" beats "training".
- **Outcome-shaped.** Name the deliverable by what it produces, not what it is. "Customer-acquisition playbook" beats "PDF guide".
- **Bundled at the right grain.** Too granular (37 separate deliverables) feels chaotic. Too coarse ("the program") feels vague. Aim for 3–7 named chunks.
- **Delivered, not made.** "You get X" beats "We build X for you" unless the latter is the explicit promise.

### Failure modes

- **Generic deliverable** ("coaching", "training", "support"). What does that mean concretely?
- **Curriculum-shaped** — listing 47 modules. The buyer doesn't care about modules; they care about the result.
- **Buried lead** — the actual deliverable is item 7 of 12. Lead with it.

### One-sentence test

> The buyer reads the deliverable sentence and can describe it to a friend in their own words.

---

## 2. Bonus Stack

**Question:** What else comes with it, and why does each bonus exist?

### What makes a good bonus stack

- **Each bonus earns its place.** Every bonus must do one of: accelerate time to result, increase perceived likelihood, reduce effort, or expand the dream outcome.
- **Real value, not inflated value.** State the value honestly. "$297 template pack" beats "$2,997 BONUS!!!" if the pack is genuinely a $297 pack.
- **Stacked around a real gap.** Look at the buyer's journey from purchase to result. The bonuses should fill the gaps.
- **3–6 bonuses.** Fewer than 3 looks thin. More than 6 starts to feel like theater.

### Failure modes

- **Inflation theater.** Stacking 12 bonuses with inflated values to manufacture a "total value" number that doesn't survive scrutiny.
- **Bonus for bonus's sake.** Adding bonuses the buyer wouldn't pay for separately.
- **No reason for each bonus.** If you can't explain why the bonus is there in one sentence, cut it.

### One-sentence test

> Every bonus has a stated reason: "This bonus exists to [accelerate result / reduce effort / etc.]."

See `bonus-stacking.md` for the full stacking discipline.

## 3. Guarantee

**Question:** How is risk removed or reversed?

### What makes a good guarantee

- **Real.** It has teeth — the operator will actually pay out.
- **Specific.** Names the condition, the refund window, and what "success" looks like.
- **Mismatched to risk.** The guarantee should remove MORE risk than the buyer is taking. A 30-day refund on a $5,000 program is fine. A 30-day refund on a $200 info product is overkill.
- **Aligned to outcome.** The guarantee should trigger if the buyer does the work and doesn't get the result.

### 8 guarantee types

Full deep dive in `guarantee-design.md`. Quick reference:

1. **Unconditional money-back** — any reason, any time, full refund.
2. **Conditional money-back** — refund if specific conditions met (e.g., "complete the work and don't get the result").
3. **Performance guarantee** — refund if a specific metric isn't hit.
4. **Time-based guarantee** — "if you don't see X in Y days, full refund".
5. **Milestone guarantee** — refund at a specific milestone if outcomes are off-track.
6. **Partial guarantee** — partial refund if conditions partially met.
7. **Rolling-cohort guarantee** — "join the next cohort free if you're not satisfied".
8. **Anti-guarantee** — explicitly state there's no guarantee, frame why (rare, position-dependent).

### Failure modes

- **Boilerplate guarantee.** "30-day money-back guarantee" with no specifics.
- **Guarantee that doesn't match the offer.** A 30-day refund on a 6-month program doesn't align.
- **Hidden terms.** Refund buried in fine print or with conditions that make it un-claimable.
- **No guarantee.** For offers above $500 with high effort, missing guarantee is a Perceived Likelihood killer.

### One-sentence test

> The buyer reads the guarantee and believes the operator will pay out.

## 4. Scarcity / Urgency

**Question:** Why buy now and not later?

### What makes good scarcity

- **Real.** Backed by an actual constraint — cohort cap, calendar window, asset limit, partner limit, or evergreen trigger.
- **Specific.** Not "limited spots" — "this cohort closes Friday at 5pm ET and the next one is Q1 2027".
- **Buyer-relevant.** The buyer cares about the constraint, not the operator's schedule.
- **Aligned.** Scarcity should serve the buyer's outcome, not just the operator's revenue.

### 5 scarcity frameworks

Full deep dive in `scarcity-frameworks.md`. Quick reference:

1. **Cohort cap** — "12 founders per cohort, doors close Friday".
2. **Calendar window** — "Q1 enrollment, next window Q3".
3. **Evergreen trigger** — "Starts the first Monday after you join".
4. **Asset-limited** — "First 50 buyers get the template pack; rest don't".
5. **Partner-limited** — "Bundled with X partner until Y date, then unavailable".

### Failure modes

- **Fake countdown timers.** Evergreen "X hours left" with no real deadline. Buyers can tell.
- **Manufactured scarcity.** "Only 3 spots" when there are 30.
- **Scarcity that doesn't serve the buyer.** "Buy in the next 10 minutes for 50% off" with no reason.
- **No scarcity.** Evergreen offers with no urgency convert at 20–30% of cohort offers.

### One-sentence test

> The buyer understands the constraint and believes it's real.

## 5. Name

**Question:** What's it called?

### What makes a good name

- **Ownable.** Specific enough that the buyer can ask for it by name.
- **Outcome-shaped.** Carries the result, not the format. "The 90-Day MRR Sprint" beats "The 12-Week Course".
- **Specific to the buyer.** Names the audience or their situation. "Founder's Pricing Audit" beats "Pricing Strategy".
- **Memorable.** Short enough to remember, distinct enough to find.

### Naming patterns

| Pattern | Example | When it works |
|---------|---------|---------------|
| **Time-bound outcome** | "The 90-Day MRR Sprint" | Clear timeline + clear outcome |
| **Audience-shaped** | "Founder's Pricing Audit" | Buyer names themselves in the title |
| **Mechanism-named** | "The 4-Block Pricing Method" | Differentiated by approach, not just result |
| **Cohort-numbered** | "Maven Cohort 7" | When the cohort itself is a brand |
| **Outcome-named** | "Predictable Revenue" | Aspirational, often paired with mechanism |

### Failure modes

- **Generic.** "Coaching Program", "Masterclass", "Course". Buyer can't differentiate.
- **Clever-for-clever's-sake.** Names that require decoding. "The Phoenix Protocol" with no context.
- **Format-shaped.** Names that describe the format, not the result. "The 8-Week Live Cohort Course".

### One-sentence test

> The buyer can text the name to a friend and the friend knows what it is.

## 6. Price + Payment Structure

**Question:** How much, paid how?

### What makes good pricing

- **Anchored.** A reference price (full value, premium tier, or "if you hired an agency") so the actual price reads as fair.
- **Aligned to outcome.** Higher outcome = higher price. If the outcome is "add $40k MRR", $5,000 is a no-brainer; if it's "feel more confident", $5,000 is unanchored.
- **Payment structure matched to buyer cashflow.** Monthly for B2B, lump-sum for individuals, milestone payments for projects.
- **Premium for full pay.** A 5–15% discount for paying in full is standard. Bigger discounts cannibalize the lump-sum option.

### Payment structures

| Structure | When it works |
|-----------|---------------|
| **One-time** | Info products, courses, project-based services |
| **Monthly recurring** | Retainers, subscriptions, ongoing coaching |
| **Multi-pay** | Higher-ticket offers where the buyer can't pay lump-sum ($3k+ courses, $10k+ coaching) |
| **Milestone** | Project-based services (50% start, 50% delivery) |
| **Performance** | Agency / consulting where a base fee + upside makes sense |
| **Custom** | High-ticket B2B, enterprise — proposal-based |

### Failure modes

- **Unanchored price.** "$5,000" alone reads as expensive. Anchored against a $25,000 alternative, it reads as a deal.
- **No payment plan.** Forces buyer to lump-sum or leave. Many will leave.
- **Multi-pay with no premium for lump-sum.** Buyers choose the longer plan because there's no reason not to.
- **Price changes without justification.** Discounting for no reason trains buyers to wait.

### One-sentence test

> The buyer reads the price and understands (a) why it costs that much, (b) how they can pay, and (c) why paying now is better than later.

See `/pricing` for anchor analysis, elasticity, and pricing math. See `/launch` for how price points interact with launch sequence.

## Worked example — all six components

**Offer:** Coaching for founders who want to add $30k MRR in 90 days.

| # | Component | Spec |
|---|-----------|------|
| 1 | **Core deliverable** | Four 60-minute strategy calls (one every 3 weeks), async Loom reviews between calls, a written pricing + outbound plan by Day 14. |
| 2 | **Bonus stack** | (a) Pricing diagnostic template ($297), (b) Outbound swipe file of 30 high-converting cold emails ($197), (c) Group office hours every other week (included). |
| 3 | **Guarantee** | Conditional: if you complete all four calls and the homework and don't add at least $15k MRR in 90 days, full refund. |
| 4 | **Scarcity** | Cohort cap of 8 founders. Next cohort opens in 14 weeks. |
| 5 | **Name** | "The 90-Day MRR Sprint" |
| 6 | **Price** | $7,500 one-time, or 3 × $2,750 (saves $750). Anchored against "$25,000 agency engagement" stated as a comparison in the copy. |

Anti-pattern gate (Stage 5): No fake timers, bonuses stated at real value, no banned hype, guarantee is specific and aligned, scarcity is real. **PASS.**

This is a $7,500 offer. It earned its price by addressing the leak (Dream Outcome + Perceived Likelihood), with a guarantee that aligns risk and a cohort cap that creates real urgency.
