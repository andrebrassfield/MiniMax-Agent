# Bonus Stacking — Principles

A bonus stack accelerates time to result, increases perceived likelihood, reduces effort, or expands the dream outcome. Anything else is theater.

The discipline isn't which bonuses to include — it's which bonuses to **cut**.

---

## The principle

Each bonus must do one of these four jobs, and you must be able to name which:

1. **Accelerate time to result.** "Get to the first win in 7 days, not 30."
2. **Increase perceived likelihood.** "Here's proof you can do this even if you've failed before."
3. **Reduce effort.** "We do X for you, so you don't have to."
4. **Expand the dream outcome.** "Beyond the core promise, you also get Y."

If a bonus doesn't do one of these four jobs, cut it.

---

## What a bonus is NOT

- **A bonus for bonus's sake.** "Bonus: weekly newsletter" — not a bonus. Just content.
- **A repurposed asset.** "Bonus: 4-hour workshop recording from 2023" — not a bonus. Inventory dump.
- **An inflated-value placeholder.** "$2,997 BONUS!" for a 30-minute template — inflation theater.
- **A format change.** "Bonus: PDF version of the course" — not a bonus. The course in a different format.

---

## Real value vs perceived value vs inflated value

This is the most common anti-pattern. Bonuses must state **real or honestly perceived value**, never inflated value.

| Type | Example | Verdict |
|------|---------|---------|
| **Real value** | "Pricing diagnostic template — $297 value" (template genuinely worth $297 standalone) | OK |
| **Perceived value** | "Group office hours — value: priceless" or "value: $1,500" (genuinely worth attending, hard to price) | OK if framed honestly |
| **Inflated value** | "$2,997 BONUS!!!" for a 30-minute template the operator made in an afternoon | Anti-pattern. Buyers can do the math. |

**The rule:** if the buyer could buy the bonus separately at the stated price, would they? If yes, the value is real. If no, it's inflated.

---

## How to build a real stack

Start with the buyer's journey from purchase to result. List every gap between "I bought this" and "I got the outcome." Each gap is a candidate for a bonus.

**Example buyer journey:**
1. Buys offer
2. Schedules onboarding call
3. Gets access to materials
4. Tries to apply what they learned
5. Hits confusion
6. Tries again
7. Starts seeing results
8. Sustains results

**Gaps that could be bonuses:**
- Step 2 → 3: "Onboarding checklist" (accelerates time to result)
- Step 4 → 5: "Office hours" (reduces effort)
- Step 5 → 6: "Decision tree template" (reduces effort)
- Step 7: "90-day sustainment plan" (expands outcome)

Each bonus is named, has a stated value, and does one of the four jobs. The total stack supports the journey.

---

## How many bonuses

| Number | Verdict |
|--------|---------|
| 0 | Looks thin for any offer above $500. |
| 1–2 | Light. OK for high-ticket offers where the core deliverable is heavy. |
| 3–5 | Sweet spot for most offers. |
| 6–8 | Upper limit. Anything more starts to feel like theater. |
| 9+ | Almost always inflation. Cut. |

---

## Stacking patterns that work

### The "speeds you up" stack
1. Quick-start template (accelerates time to result)
2. Onboarding checklist (accelerates time to result)
3. Group office hours (reduces effort)

### The "de-risks the journey" stack
1. Decision-tree template (reduces effort)
2. FAQ document covering top 20 questions (reduces effort)
3. "If you get stuck" troubleshooting guide (reduces effort)

### The "expands the outcome" stack
1. Sustainment plan (expands outcome)
2. Advanced playbook for after the core result (expands outcome)
3. Community access for ongoing questions (increases perceived likelihood)

These are stacks, not random bonuses. Each one supports a specific job.

---

## Anti-patterns to avoid

### Anti-pattern: "$X,YYY total value"
Stacking bonuses to manufacture a "total value" number that's higher than the offer price. Buyers can do the math. If the bonuses are genuinely worth $5k total and the offer is $2k, state the math. If they're not, don't.

### Anti-pattern: "Bonus: my $5,000 course"
Adding the operator's own existing high-ticket product as a "bonus" to a lower-ticket offer. This cannibalizes the high-ticket offer. If the high-ticket offer exists, don't bundle it as a bonus to a lower tier.

### Anti-pattern: "Bonus: 1 hour with me"
A coaching call as a bonus on a $200 product. Either the call is valuable (then it's not a bonus — it's the product) or it's not (then it's a token gesture).

### Anti-pattern: Mystery bonuses
"Unannounced bonus when you join." If the bonus isn't named, the buyer can't evaluate it. Don't use mystery as a sales tactic — it reads as filler.

---

## When bonuses hurt

Sometimes the best move is fewer bonuses or none. Consider this when:

- The core deliverable is already strong and heavy. Adding bonuses dilutes it.
- The offer is high-ticket ($10k+) where bonuses feel incongruent with the price.
- The operator can't deliver on the bonuses with quality. A bad bonus is worse than no bonus.
- The bonuses are doing theater instead of work.

A $25k consulting engagement with five "$497 bonuses" looks unserious. A $200 info product with five real bonuses looks generous. Match the stack to the offer.
