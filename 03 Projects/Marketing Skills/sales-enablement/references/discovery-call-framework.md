# Discovery Call Framework — SPIN-Style Questions

The discovery call is where the buyer qualifies themselves (or doesn't). Most operators treat discovery as a checkbox before the pitch. The discipline is the opposite: discovery IS the value. The buyer who feels heard is the buyer who closes.

---

## The SPIN framework (adapted)

Adapted from Neil Rackham's SPIN Selling for high-ticket service offers.

**S — Situation:** Where are they now?
**P — Problem:** What's broken?
**I — Implication:** What does staying stuck cost?
**N — Need-payoff:** What would solving this be worth?

The progression matters. Situation questions establish context. Problem questions surface pain. Implication questions make the cost vivid. Need-payoff questions get the buyer to articulate the value of solving it themselves — which is more persuasive than any pitch.

---

## Question categories

### Situation questions (3–5)

**Goal:** Understand the buyer's current state. Don't assume — ask.

**Examples:**
- "Walk me through your current [process/system/situation]."
- "How are you handling [X] today?"
- "What's your team look like for this?"
- "How long has this been a priority?"
- "What have you tried so far?"

**Anti-pattern:** Asking situation questions to demonstrate you understand the buyer's world. They feel patronized.

**Rule:** Keep situation questions short. The buyer should answer in 30–60 seconds each. If they're answering for 5 minutes, you're in Problem territory.

---

### Problem questions (3–5)

**Goal:** Surface the specific pain. Make the buyer articulate what's broken.

**Examples:**
- "What's the biggest challenge with [X] right now?"
- "Where does [process] break down?"
- "What would you change if you could change one thing?"
- "What's frustrating you most about [current state]?"
- "What's the gap between where you are and where you want to be?"

**Anti-pattern:** Asking problem questions you've already inferred. The buyer wants to feel heard, not analyzed.

**Rule:** Problem questions should be open-ended. Avoid yes/no.

---

### Implication questions (2–3)

**Goal:** Make the cost of staying stuck vivid. Quantify when possible.

**Examples:**
- "What does staying at [current state] cost you over the next 6 months?"
- "If this doesn't get fixed, what happens to [metric they care about]?"
- "How is [problem] affecting [other part of business]?"
- "What else can't you do because of [problem]?"

**Why implication questions matter:** Buyers minimize their own pain until you make them articulate the cost. The buyer who says "this costs us $30k/month in lost revenue" is the buyer who closes.

**Anti-pattern:** Implying the cost for them. "So this is costing you $30k?" reads as manipulation. Let them say it.

**Rule:** Implication questions should be specific. "What's this costing you?" is too vague. "What's this costing you per month in [specific metric]?" is better.

---

### Need-payoff questions (2–3)

**Goal:** Get the buyer to articulate the value of solving the problem. The buyer who states the value out loud is the buyer who's pre-sold.

**Examples:**
- "If you could solve [problem], what would that be worth?"
- "What would change for [stakeholder] if you had [outcome]?"
- "How would [outcome] affect [metric they care about]?"
- "What would you do with the result once you had it?"

**Why need-payoff questions matter:** The buyer is now selling themselves. They've articulated the value of solving the problem. The close becomes "we deliver that value" — not "let me convince you."

**Anti-pattern:** Need-payoff questions that lead the buyer. "Wouldn't it be great if you could add $40k MRR?" reads as manipulation. "What would adding $40k MRR mean for your business?" lets them articulate.

**Rule:** Need-payoff questions should be open. Let the buyer fill in the specifics.

## Discovery question sequence

A 30-minute discovery call uses 8–12 questions in this order:

| Time | Question type | Count | Goal |
|------|---------------|-------|------|
| 0–5 min | Connect | 0 questions | Build rapport |
| 5–15 min | Situation + Problem | 4–6 questions | Understand state + pain |
| 15–22 min | Implication | 2–3 questions | Make cost vivid |
| 22–28 min | Need-payoff | 2–3 questions | Get buyer to articulate value |
| 28–30 min | Transition to prescribe | 0 questions | Move to offer |

The buyer should be talking 70%+ of the time. If they're talking 50% or less, you're pitching in disguise.

## What to listen for

### Fit signals (positive)

- Buyer has a specific, measurable problem.
- Buyer has budget or authority to buy.
- Buyer's timeline is within your fulfillment window.
- Buyer has tried to solve it (and failed).
- Buyer is comparing you to alternatives.

### Mis-fit signals (negative)

- Buyer wants generic advice without commitment.
- Buyer is "just looking" with no urgency.
- Buyer has no budget or authority.
- Buyer wants results without effort.
- Buyer is shopping for the cheapest option.

Mis-fit isn't failure — it's triage. Better to disqualify than close a deal that fails.

## Disqualifying gracefully

Not every discovery call leads to a sale. Disqualifying is a feature.

**Phrases:**
- "Based on what you've shared, I'm not sure this is the right fit. Here's what I'd recommend instead..."
- "What you're describing sounds like [different solution]. Want an intro?"
- "We're probably not the right team for this. Let me think about who would be."

Disqualifying gracefully builds reputation. The buyer you referred elsewhere remembers and refers back.

## Discovery call anti-patterns

### Pitching during discovery
The buyer shares their problem. The operator jumps to "we can solve that." The buyer feels sold, not helped.

**Replacement:** Listen fully. Diagnose. Then prescribe.

### Asking questions you could have inferred
"Tell me about your business" when their website is right there. The buyer feels patronized.

**Replacement:** Show you've done your homework. Ask the deeper questions.

### Leading questions
"Wouldn't you say this is hurting your revenue?" The buyer feels manipulated.

**Replacement:** Open questions. Let the buyer fill in the specifics.

### Implying the cost for them
"So this is costing you $30k/month, right?" The buyer gets defensive.

**Replacement:** Ask "What is this costing you per month?" Let them quantify.

### Multi-part questions
"What's your timeline, budget, and who's involved in the decision?" The buyer doesn't know where to start.

**Replacement:** One question at a time. Wait for the answer.

### Interrupting
The buyer is mid-sentence. The operator jumps in with a related question.

**Replacement:** Wait. Let the buyer finish. Then respond.

### Talking more than the buyer
If you've talked more than 30% of the call, you've pitched too much.

**Replacement:** Aim for 70%+ buyer talk time.

## Listening techniques

### Paraphrase

> "So what I'm hearing is [X]. Is that right?"

**Why it works:** Confirms understanding. The buyer feels heard.

### Reflect

> "It sounds like [emotion: frustration, overwhelm, excitement]. Does that resonate?"

**Why it works:** Names the emotion. The buyer feels seen.

### Summarize

> "Let me make sure I've got this: [summary]. Did I miss anything?"

**Why it works:** Catches gaps. Sets up the prescribe phase.

### Pause

After the buyer finishes a thought, pause for 3–5 seconds before responding.

**Why it works:** The buyer often adds the most important detail in the pause.

### Don't fill silence

If the buyer pauses to think, don't jump in. Let them fill it.

**Why it works:** Silence surfaces the buyer's real concerns.

## Discovery question bank (25 questions)

Pull from these based on the buyer's situation:

**Situation:**
1. "Walk me through your current [process]."
2. "How are you handling [X] today?"
3. "How big is your team?"
4. "How long has this been a priority?"
5. "What tools are you using for [X]?"

**Problem:**
6. "What's the biggest challenge with [X] right now?"
7. "Where does [process] break down?"
8. "What would you change if you could change one thing?"
9. "What's frustrating you most about [current state]?"
10. "What's the gap between where you are and where you want to be?"

**Implication:**
11. "What does staying at [current state] cost you over the next 6 months?"
12. "If this doesn't get fixed, what happens to [metric]?"
13. "How is [problem] affecting [other part of business]?"
14. "What else can't you do because of [problem]?"
15. "What does the delay cost you?"

**Need-payoff:**
16. "If you could solve [problem], what would that be worth?"
17. "What would change for [stakeholder] if you had [outcome]?"
18. "How would [outcome] affect [metric]?"
19. "What would you do with the result once you had it?"
20. "Why does solving this matter now?"

**Closing:**
21. "If we could deliver [outcome], would you be ready to move forward?"
22. "What would need to be true for you to say yes today?"
23. "Is there anything else I should know?"
24. "Who else needs to be involved in this decision?"
25. "What's your timeline for getting this solved?"

## Quick checklist

Before running a discovery call:

- [ ] 10–15 questions prepared, in SPIN order
- [ ] Buyer profile reviewed (their website, social, past emails)
- [ ] Fit criteria clear (when to disqualify)
- [ ] Listening techniques practiced (paraphrase, reflect, summarize)
- [ ] Notes template ready (capture key facts + quotes)
- [ ] Transition to prescribe prepared ("based on what you've shared...")
- [ ] Anti-pitch reminders (70%+ buyer talk time)
