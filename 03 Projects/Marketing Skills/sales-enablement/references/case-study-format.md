# Case Study Format — Before / After / Bridge

Case studies move Perceived Likelihood. The buyer who sees someone like them get the result is the buyer who closes. The discipline: case studies are specific, structured, and match the buyer's situation.

---

## The Before / After / Bridge format

The most effective case study structure has three parts:

### Before

**The buyer's situation before working with you.**

- Who they were (specific role, company, context).
- What they were trying to do.
- What was blocking them.
- What they'd tried before.
- Quantified state (revenue, time, team size, whatever matters).

**Why it matters:** The buyer needs to see themselves in the "before." If the case study is too far from their situation, it doesn't move them.

### After

**The result they got.**

- The specific outcome (with number).
- The timeframe it took.
- What changed for them.
- Quantified result (the bigger the number, the better — but it has to be real).

**Why it matters:** The "after" is what the buyer is buying. Make it specific enough that they can picture it.

### Bridge

**How they got from before to after. The mechanism.**

- What specifically was done (the offer, the work).
- The key moments or decisions.
- Why it worked (the mechanism, in plain terms).

**Why it matters:** The buyer needs to believe THEY can take the same bridge. The mechanism has to be understandable and reproducible.

---

## Anatomy of a good case study

### Length

**Short case study (200–400 words):** For emails, sales calls, quick reference.

**Medium case study (500–800 words):** For the sales page, proposal, longer emails.

**Long case study (1,000–2,000 words):** For dedicated case study pages, content marketing.

Default to medium. Short if the case study is one of many.

### Structure

```markdown
# How [Buyer Name] [Outcome] in [Timeframe]

## Before
[Their situation, specific. 2–4 sentences.]

## After
[Their result, specific. 2–4 sentences with quantified outcome.]

## Bridge
[How they got there. 3–6 sentences explaining the mechanism.]
```

### Pull quote

End with a direct quote from the buyer (with permission).

> "I was at $3k MRR and burning out on cold email. Three months into the Sprint, we're at $22k MRR. The pricing audit alone was worth 10x the engagement fee."
> — Sarah K., Founder, Lumen Analytics

The quote is the most-shared element. Make it specific and pull-worthy.

## Worked example

### Before

Marcus was at $0 MRR with a $50/mo product nobody was buying. He'd launched six months earlier, gotten 200 email subscribers, and converted exactly three of them. He'd tried cold email (40% bounce rate), Twitter threads (no engagement), and a Product Hunt launch (30 upvotes). His runway was 8 months. He was about to start looking for a job.

### After

In 60 days, Marcus rebuilt his offer (added a $500/mo tier), his pricing page (moved from monthly to annual with a 20% discount), and his close sequence (added a 14-day trial instead of asking for the sale upfront). By Day 60, he was at $8,200 MRR — from $0 — with the same product, the same audience, and a different mechanism.

### Bridge

The mechanism was the 4-Block Pricing Method: pricing structure, payment structure, price presentation, and close sequence. Marcus had been selling monthly with no trial, no annual option, and a single-tier structure. We rebuilt all four blocks in 60 days. The key insight: his audience had been waiting for an annual option (lower friction, lower churn) and a trial (lower commitment). He hadn't known because he'd never asked.

### Pull quote

> "I thought the problem was marketing. It was pricing. The Sprint fixed it in 60 days."
> — Marcus T., Founder, [Company]

## Case study anti-patterns

### Generic case study
"We helped them grow their business." No specifics, no numbers, no name.

### Too far from the buyer's situation
Case study of a Fortune 500 company. Buyer is a solopreneur. Doesn't move them.

### Stock-photo headshot next to fake quote
Made-up testimonials with stock photos. Buyers can tell.

### All before, no after
"The buyer was struggling with X, Y, Z." No result. Doesn't move Perceived Likelihood.

### All after, no before
"The buyer got amazing results." Buyer doesn't see themselves in the case.

### Mechanism explained in jargon
"We used our proprietary framework to optimize their funnel." Vague. Buyer doesn't understand the bridge.

### No pull quote
Case study without a memorable line. Less shareable.

### Outdated case study
3-year-old case study from before the offer changed. Buyer wonders if it's still relevant.

### Negative outcome framed as positive
"We learned a lot." The buyer wanted the result. Failures don't make good case studies (unless reframed as learning).

## How many case studies to have

| Sales volume | Recommended case studies |
|--------------|--------------------------|
| Low-ticket (<$500) | 1–2 testimonials on the sales page |
| Mid-ticket ($500–$5k) | 3–5 case studies |
| High-ticket ($5k–$25k) | 5–10 case studies (with 2–3 deep dives) |
| Very high-ticket ($25k+) | 10+ case studies (with multiple matching profiles) |

Match case study count to sales volume. More case studies for longer buyer journeys.

## How to collect case studies

### After the buyer gets the result

Reach out 30–60 days after delivery. Ask for a 15-min call to capture their story.

**Sample email:**

> Hey [name],
>
> It's been [X weeks] since we wrapped up. I'd love to capture your story for [specific purpose — case study, sales page, etc.].
>
> Would you be open to a 15-min call? I'll ask a few questions about your before / after. With your permission, I'll write it up and send it to you for approval before using it anywhere.
>
> As a thank-you, [small incentive — extended support, referral credit, etc.].
>
> [Calendar link]

### Ask specific questions on the call

- "Where were you before we started?"
- "What had you tried before?"
- "What was the cost of staying stuck?"
- "What changed after [offer]?"
- "What would you tell someone considering [offer]?"

### Get approval in writing

After writing up the case study, send it to the buyer for approval before using it anywhere. They should sign off on the wording, the quote, and the use.

---

## When to update case studies

- When the offer changes significantly.
- When the buyer's profile shifts (e.g., from SMB to mid-market).
- When the case study is more than 12 months old.
- When new case studies are better matches for the buyer's situation.

A 5-year-old case study is still useful, but a recent case study with a matching profile is more persuasive.

## Case studies in different contexts

### Sales page

**Use:** 2–3 case studies, matching different buyer profiles.

**Format:** Pull quotes + 2–4 sentence summaries. Link to deeper case study if needed.

### Email

**Use:** 1 case study, focused on the email's specific angle.

**Format:** 200–400 words. Pull quote. Link to fuller story if relevant.

### Sales call

**Use:** 1–2 case studies matching the buyer's profile.

**Format:** Verbal summary (2–3 min). "Here's a buyer in a similar situation..." Then walk through before/after/bridge.

### Proposal

**Use:** 1–2 case studies matching the buyer's profile.

**Format:** 200–400 words. Pull quote. As a section in the proposal.

### Cold outreach

**Use:** 1 brief case study as proof.

**Format:** 50–100 words. Just the after result + pull quote.

## Quick checklist

Before using a case study:

- [ ] Buyer profile matches the target buyer's situation
- [ ] Before is specific (situation, what they tried, quantified state)
- [ ] After is specific (result, timeframe, quantified outcome)
- [ ] Bridge explains the mechanism (understandable, reproducible)
- [ ] Pull quote is memorable and pull-worthy
- [ ] Buyer has approved the wording (in writing)
- [ ] Buyer has approved the use (in writing)
- [ ] Case study is recent enough to be relevant
- [ ] Case study isn't too far from the buyer's situation
- [ ] No fake testimonials or stock photos
