# Proposal Template — Standard Sections + Customization

The proposal is sent after the call. It mirrors the call's structure (their problem, your solution) and adds the price, terms, and next steps. The proposal is not a sales pitch — it's a recap the buyer can review and forward to stakeholders.

---

## Standard sections

A high-ticket proposal has 6 sections in this order:

1. **Executive summary** (1 paragraph)
2. **The situation** (their problem, in their words)
3. **The cost of inaction** (what staying stuck costs)
4. **The proposed approach** (your offer as the bridge)
5. **The investment** (price + structure + guarantee)
6. **Next steps** (clear, time-bound)

Total length: 1,500–2,500 words. Anything longer, the buyer doesn't read.

---

## Section-by-section

### 1. Executive summary

**Goal:** Restate the buyer's situation and your proposed solution in 3–5 sentences.

**Template:**

> [Buyer name / company] is at [their stated situation]. The cost of staying at this state is [quantified cost over timeframe]. [Your offer name] delivers [outcome] in [timeframe] for [price].
>
> This proposal outlines the engagement, the investment, and the next steps.

**Why it matters:** The buyer (and any stakeholder) reads the executive summary first. It should be skimmable.

---

### 2. The situation

**Goal:** Restate the buyer's problem in their words. Show you heard them.

**Template:**

> Based on our conversation on [date], here's what we understood about your situation:
>
> - [Their stated problem #1, in their words]
> - [Their stated problem #2, in their words]
> - [Their stated context, in their words]
>
> You've tried [what they've tried], and the gap between where you are and where you want to be is [their stated gap].

**Why it matters:** The buyer forwards this to stakeholders. The stakeholders need to see "this person understood us."

**Anti-pattern:** Generic situation. The buyer doesn't feel heard.

---

### 3. The cost of inaction

**Goal:** Make the cost of staying stuck vivid. The buyer articulated this on the call; restate it.

**Template:**

> If [problem] stays where it is for the next [timeframe], here's what that costs:
>
> - [Quantified cost #1, in their words]
> - [Quantified cost #2, in their words]
> - [Implication cost — what else can't they do]

**Why it matters:** The cost of inaction makes the investment feel smaller. The buyer who sees $30k/month in lost revenue closes on $7,500.

**Anti-pattern:** Implying the cost for them. Use what they said.

---

### 4. The proposed approach

**Goal:** Present the offer as the mechanism that delivers what they said they wanted.

**Template:**

> [Offer name] delivers [outcome] in [timeframe]. Here's the approach:
>
> **Phase 1: [Name]** ([timeframe])
> - [Specific deliverable]
> - [Specific deliverable]
>
> **Phase 2: [Name]** ([timeframe])
> - [Specific deliverable]
> - [Specific deliverable]
>
> **Phase 3: [Name]** ([timeframe])
> - [Specific deliverable]
> - [Specific deliverable]
>
> **Bonus / included:**
> - [Bonus 1, with stated value]
> - [Bonus 2, with stated value]
>
> **Total value:** $X
> **Your investment:** $Y (see below)

**Why it matters:** Phases show the buyer what happens and when. Bonus shows the offer is complete.

**Anti-pattern:** Listing deliverables without showing the path. The buyer doesn't see how they get from problem to outcome.

---

### 5. The investment

**Goal:** State the price with anchor. State the payment structure. State the guarantee.

**Template:**

> **Investment:** $X (one-time) or [payment plan] ($Y × N).
>
> **Anchored comparison:** Most [buyers in their situation] pay $Z for [alternative]. We deliver the same outcome in [shorter timeframe] for $X.
>
> **Guarantee:** [Guarantee type and terms]. [What triggers a refund. What's the window.]

**Why it matters:** The anchor makes the price feel fair. The guarantee removes risk. Both are needed.

**Anti-pattern:** Price without anchor. "It's $7,500" alone reads as expensive.

---

### 6. Next steps

**Goal:** Tell the buyer exactly what to do to move forward.

**Template:**

> **To move forward:**
> 1. Reply to this email with "Yes, let's go" by [date].
> 2. I'll send the invoice within 24 hours.
> 3. We kick off on [date].
> 4. First deliverable: [X] by [date].
>
> **Questions?** Reply to this email or book a 15-min call here: [link].
>
> **Not the right fit?** No problem. If [alternative] would serve you better, here's who I'd recommend: [name + intro].

**Why it matters:** Clear next steps remove friction. The buyer knows exactly what to do.

**Anti-pattern:** Vague next steps. "Let me know what you think" leaves the buyer hanging.

## Proposal format

**Format:** PDF + email body. The PDF is the formal artifact. The email body is the conversational wrapper.

**PDF design:**
- Clean, branded, but not over-designed.
- 1.5 line spacing for readability.
- Headers for each section.
- Footer with contact info + signature.
- 2–4 pages total.

**Email body:**
- 100–200 words.
- "Attached is the proposal we discussed on [date]."
- 2–3 sentence summary of the offer.
- Link to book a follow-up call if needed.

---

## Customization per buyer

The proposal should NOT be a generic template. Customize for each buyer.

**Customize:**
- Executive summary (their situation + your solution).
- The situation section (their words).
- The cost of inaction (their numbers).
- The proposed approach (their priorities).
- The next steps (their timeline).

**Don't customize:**
- The six components of the offer (these are standard).
- The price (unless negotiated).
- The guarantee (these are standard).

The "their words" sections get rewritten. The "your offer" sections stay.

---

## When to send the proposal

**Send within 24 hours of the call.** Buyers cool off fast. Same-day is best.

**Send to the buyer + any stakeholders they mentioned.** If they said "I'll need to talk to my CFO," copy the CFO on the proposal.

**Send from a real person.** "Sarah from [Brand]" > "The [Brand] Team." Personal > institutional.

---

## What NOT to include in the proposal

- Generic case studies that don't match the buyer's situation.
- Long bios of the operator's credentials.
- Detailed methodology (they don't need to know how you do what you do — just what they get).
- Pricing of tiers they didn't ask about.
- Anything that wasn't discussed on the call.

The proposal is a recap, not an information dump.

---

## Proposal anti-patterns

### Generic template
Same proposal for every buyer. The buyer feels like one of many.

**Replacement:** Customize the executive summary, situation, cost of inaction, and next steps.

### Long proposal (5+ pages)
The buyer doesn't read. Key info is buried.

**Replacement:** 2–4 pages. Key info above the fold.

### Pitch-heavy proposal
"Why we're the best" + "our unique approach" + "what makes us different." Buyer doesn't care.

**Replacement:** Recap of their situation + your solution + the price.

### No next steps
"Let me know what you think." No clear action.

**Replacement:** Specific next steps with dates.

### Price without anchor
"$7,500" alone. Reads as expensive.

**Replacement:** Anchor against alternative cost.

### Hidden fees
Price on the proposal is $7,500. Invoice shows $7,800. Buyer feels tricked.

**Replacement:** Total price, all-in.

### No guarantee
Proposal doesn't mention the guarantee. Buyer doesn't see risk removal.

**Replacement:** Guarantee stated explicitly in the investment section.

### Sending before the call
Sending a generic proposal to a cold lead. Buyer doesn't have context.

**Replacement:** Always have a call first (for high-ticket).

### Not following up
Sending the proposal and waiting. Most proposals don't close without follow-up.

**Replacement:** Follow-up sequence (see `follow-up-sequences.md`).
