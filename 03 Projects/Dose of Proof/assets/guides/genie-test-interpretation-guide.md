## Section 1 — What the GENIE test actually measures

The GENIE test is a transcriptomics panel that uses **NanoString nCounter** technology to measure the messenger RNA (mRNA) expression of 188 genes directly from a peripheral blood sample.

Here's what that means in plain language:

**DNA** is the blueprint. It's the same in nearly every cell of your body. It doesn't change based on what's happening to you right now.

**mRNA** is the active instruction set. When a gene is "expressed," the cell makes an mRNA copy of that gene and uses it as a template to build a protein. The amount of mRNA floating around tells you how actively that gene is being read.

**Proteins** are what actually do the work — enzymes, receptors, structural components, signaling molecules.

Standard blood tests measure proteins (C4a, TGF-beta-1, MMP-9, hormones, etc.). The GENIE test measures mRNA. Both are useful. They answer different questions.

Standard blood tests tell you: "What protein level is in the blood right now?"
The GENIE test tells you: "How actively are these specific genes being transcribed right now?"

**Three things GENIE can tell you:**

1. **Whether a gene cluster is over- or under-expressed** compared to a reference population
2. **The pattern of expression across multiple genes in a pathway** (mitochondrial, coagulation, innate immune, etc.)
3. **How the pattern changes over time** when the test is repeated at intervals

**Three things GENIE can't tell you:**

1. **Why the expression pattern is what it is** — the test reports observations, not causes
2. **What you should do about it** — that's a clinical conversation, not a transcriptomics report, and not the brand's role
3. **Whether your pattern is "normal for you"** — the reference ranges are population statistics, not personal targets

The test is a research tool used in some CIRS / biotoxin illness clinical contexts. It is not a substitute for standard clinical workup, environmental testing, or physician judgment.

## Section 2 — How to read the color coding

The GENIE report uses a traffic-light-style color coding to make gene expression patterns visually scannable.

### The three colors

**🔵 BLUE — Down-regulated (under-expressed)**

Genes in this category show mRNA expression **below** the reference range. The cell is making less of this gene's mRNA than the comparison population.

In the CIRS / biotoxin context, the most clinically discussed blue cluster is the **mitochondrial hypometabolism pattern** — genes involved in cellular energy production show reduced transcription. This is interpreted by some clinicians as evidence of a Cell Danger Response (CDR), where the cell shifts from energy production toward defense.

🔵 blue doesn't mean "good" or "bad." It means "less mRNA than the reference range." Interpretation depends on which gene, which pathway, and your overall pattern.

**🔴 RED — Up-regulated (over-expressed)**

Genes in this category show mRNA expression **above** the reference range. The cell is making more of this gene's mRNA than the comparison population.

In the CIRS / biotoxin context, red clusters often include:
- Innate immune activation genes (correlated with serum C4a, TGF-beta-1, MMP-9)
- Coagulation cascade genes
- Some apoptosis / cell death signaling genes
- Some inflammatory signaling pathways

🔴 red doesn't mean "good" or "bad." It means "more mRNA than the reference range." Interpretation depends on context.

**⬜ GRAY — Within reference range**

Genes in this category show mRNA expression **within** the reference range.

Gray isn't necessarily the goal — it just means the gene expression matches the comparison population. Your personal optimal for a specific gene may differ from the population range. See Section 8 for the limits of reference-range interpretation.

### What the color pattern tells you

**Single genes are noise. Patterns are signal.**

A single blue or red gene in isolation is rarely actionable. What clinicians and researchers look for is:

- **Cluster patterns** — are multiple genes in the same pathway showing coordinated up- or down-regulation?
- **Magnitude** — how far above or below the reference range?
- **Trajectory** — are these patterns improving, holding, or worsening across repeat tests?
- **Clinical correlation** — do the patterns line up with your symptoms, your serum biomarkers, your environmental exposure history?

The GENIE report is most useful as a **trend tracker** across multiple timepoints, not as a single snapshot for decision-making.

## Section 3 — Mitochondrial hypometabolism markers (the blue cluster)

The most-discussed pattern in the CIRS / biotoxin GENIE literature is the **mitochondrial hypometabolism signature** — a cluster of genes involved in cellular energy production showing coordinated down-regulation.

### What "mitochondrial hypometabolism" means

Mitochondria are the organelles inside your cells that produce ATP — the energy currency the cell uses for nearly everything. When mitochondrial function is impaired, the cell can't produce ATP efficiently. This is interpreted by some researchers as a downstream effect of chronic innate immune activation, where the cell shifts resources from energy production toward inflammatory defense.

The Cell Danger Response (CDR) framework, developed by Dr. Robert Naviaux, describes this as a protective cellular state where the cell downregulates energy production in response to perceived threat signals (viral, bacterial, toxic, traumatic). The CDR model has been discussed in peer-reviewed literature as a unifying hypothesis for several chronic illness presentations, including chronic fatigue syndrome, mast cell activation, and post-acute infection syndromes.

**Reference:** Naviaux RK. Metabolic features of the cell danger response. *Mitochondrion.* 2014;16:7-17. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.mito.2013.08.006

### Key genes in the mitochondrial cluster (examples)

The GENIE panel includes genes like:

| Gene symbol | Function | What blue/down-regulation may suggest |
|-------------|----------|---------------------------------------|
| POLG | Mitochondrial DNA polymerase | Reduced mitochondrial DNA replication |
| TFAM | Mitochondrial transcription factor | Reduced mitochondrial gene transcription |
| COX (cytochrome c oxidase subunits) | Electron transport chain | Reduced oxidative phosphorylation capacity |
| NDUFS (NADH-ubiquinone oxidoreductase subunits) | Complex I of electron transport chain | Reduced Complex I activity |
| SDHA / SDHB | Complex II of electron transport chain | Reduced Complex II activity |
| UQCR (ubiquinol-cytochrome c reductase subunits) | Complex III | Reduced Complex III activity |
| ATP5 (ATP synthase subunits) | Complex V | Reduced ATP synthesis capacity |

### What to look for in this cluster

- **Multiple mitochondrial genes blue** = a mitochondrial hypometabolism pattern is suggested
- **Single mitochondrial gene blue** = could be noise, could be early signal
- **The pattern holds across repeat tests** = more confidence in the signal
- **The pattern is improving toward gray** = the recovery signature is starting

### Important caveat

The GENIE report tells you the mRNA expression pattern. It doesn't tell you whether your mitochondria are actually functioning differently in your tissues. mRNA is an upstream signal; protein levels and functional assays (lactate, pyruvate, organic acids) tell the downstream story.

The mitochondrial hypometabolism pattern is most useful when correlated with:
- Subjective symptoms (fatigue, brain fog, post-exertional malaise)
- Serum metabolic markers (lactate, pyruvate, fasting glucose, HbA1c)
- Lifestyle factors (sleep, movement, nutrition, exposure status)
- Your physician's clinical assessment

## Section 4 — Coagulation cascade genes (often red)

Another commonly observed pattern in CIRS / biotoxin GENIE reports is up-regulation of genes involved in the **coagulation cascade** — the molecular machinery the body uses to form blood clots.

### Why coagulation may be elevated in chronic inflammation

Chronic innate immune activation is associated with endothelial dysfunction (the cells lining blood vessels aren't working optimally) and a pro-coagulant state (the body is more ready to form clots than it should be). In CIRS literature, this has been discussed in the context of:
- Microclot formation
- Reduced fibrinolysis (the body breaks down clots more slowly)
- POTS-like symptoms (blood pooling, orthostatic intolerance)
- Endothelial inflammation markers (VEGF dysregulation, ADH dysregulation)

Recent preprint and peer-reviewed research has also described fibrin amyloid microclots in long-COVID and related chronic illness presentations, with proposed mechanisms involving complement activation and impaired fibrinolysis. The GENIE transcriptomic signature for this endothelial / coagulopathic pattern is one tool among several used to track the trajectory.

**Reference:** Pretorius E, et al. Persistent clotting protein pathology in Long COVID/Post-Acute Sequelae of COVID-19 (PASC) is accompanied by increased levels of antiplasmin. *Cardiovascular Diabetology.* 2021;20:172. https://doi.org/10.1186/s12933-021-01359-7

### Key genes in the coagulation cluster (examples)

| Gene symbol | Function | What red/up-regulation may suggest |
|-------------|----------|-------------------------------------|
| F3 (tissue factor) | Initiates extrinsic coagulation pathway | Pro-coagulant signaling |
| F5 (Factor V) | Coagulation cascade | Clot formation activity |
| FGA / FGB / FGG (fibrinogen) | Clot substrate | Fibrin formation capacity |
| SERPINE1 (PAI-1) | Inhibits fibrinolysis | Reduced clot breakdown |
| THBD (thrombomodulin) | Endothelial anticoagulant function | Endothelial status |
| VWF (von Willebrand factor) | Platelet adhesion | Endothelial activation |

### What to look for in this cluster

- **Multiple coagulation genes red** + symptoms of orthostatic intolerance, brain fog that worsens standing, slow wound healing = a coagulation / endothelial pattern may be worth tracking
- **Single coagulation gene red** = could be transient, repeat test at 90 days
- **PAI-1 up-regulated + fibrinogen up-regulated** = fibrinolytic system may be downregulated, worth tracking across repeat tests AND discussing with a physician
- **Pattern improving across repeat tests** = endothelial function may be recovering

### Important caveat

A coagulation gene pattern on the GENIE report is not a clinical diagnosis of a clotting disorder. The standard clinical workup for coagulation involves specific blood tests (PT, PTT, fibrinogen, D-dimer, etc.) and is interpreted by hematology specialists.

The GENIE coagulation pattern is most useful as a trend signal across repeat tests, correlated with your symptoms, your serum biomarkers, and your physician's clinical judgment.

## Section 5 — Innate immune activation genes (often red)

The third major cluster commonly observed in CIRS / biotoxin GENIE reports is up-regulation of genes involved in **innate immune activation** — the body's first-line, non-specific defense system.

### Why innate immune genes may be elevated

Chronic exposure to biotoxins (mold mycotoxins, Actinomycetes, endotoxins) and the resulting chronic inflammatory response can drive sustained up-regulation of innate immune genes. This pattern often correlates with the standard serum CIRS biomarkers:

The CIRS / biotoxin literature developed by Dr. Ritchie Shoemaker and colleagues describes a multi-system chronic inflammatory response triggered by biotoxin exposure in genetically susceptible individuals (HLA-DR typed). The published Shoemaker protocol outlines a stepwise diagnostic and intervention approach. The GENIE transcriptomic panel is one research tool some CIRS-literate clinicians use alongside the standard serum biomarkers to characterize the innate immune / complement activation pattern.

**Reference:** Shoemaker RC, et al. A review of the genomics of Chronic Inflammatory Response Syndrome (CIRS). *Journal of Mold and Mycotoxin Advances.* 2018;1(1):1-15. (Note: this is a foundational reference for the CIRS diagnostic framework as described in the literature; the brand does not endorse or recommend the Shoemaker protocol itself.)

| Serum biomarker | Correlated innate immune gene cluster |
|-----------------|---------------------------------------|
| C4a elevated | Complement cascade activation genes |
| TGF-beta-1 elevated | Inflammatory signaling / fibrosis genes |
| MMP-9 elevated | Matrix metalloproteinase / tissue remodeling genes |
| Tryptase elevated | Mast cell activation genes |
| IL-6, TNF-alpha elevated (when tested) | Pro-inflammatory cytokine genes |

### Key genes in the innate immune cluster (examples)

| Gene symbol | Function | What red/up-regulation may suggest |
|-------------|----------|-------------------------------------|
| C4A / C4B | Complement component 4 (correlates with serum C4a) | Complement activation |
| TGFB1 | Transforming growth factor beta-1 (correlates with serum TGF-beta-1) | Inflammatory signaling |
| MMP9 | Matrix metalloproteinase 9 (correlates with serum MMP-9) | Tissue remodeling |
| TPSAB1 / TPSB2 | Tryptase (correlates with serum tryptase) | Mast cell activity |
| IL6 | Interleukin-6 | Pro-inflammatory signaling |
| TNF | Tumor necrosis factor | Pro-inflammatory signaling |
| NFKB1 / NFKB2 | NF-kB pathway | Master inflammatory regulator |

### What to look for in this cluster

- **The innate immune pattern correlates with serum biomarkers** = the GENIE finding is supported by another measurement modality
- **The pattern is improving across repeat tests** = the chronic inflammatory burden may be decreasing
- **The pattern is worsening or holding** = environmental exposure may still be active, or upstream drivers may need attention
- **Mast cell activation genes up + serum tryptase elevated + symptoms of MCAS** = the mast cell component may be worth tracking over time

### Important caveat

Innate immune activation on the GENIE panel reflects the broader inflammatory state. It doesn't tell you which biotoxin is the trigger (mold vs Actinomycetes vs endotoxin vs other), how much exposure is ongoing, or whether your environment is "safe" now. Environmental testing (ERMI, HERTSMI-2, EnviroBiomics panels) answers the exposure question separately.

The innate immune cluster on GENIE is most useful as a trend signal correlated with:
- Standard serum CIRS biomarkers (C4a, TGF-beta-1, MMP-9)
- Symptom patterns (flushing, heat intolerance, mast cell flares)
- Environmental testing results (active exposure assessment)
- Your physician's clinical assessment

## Section 6 — How I'm tracking my own repeat tests

I'm still in this process. I haven't "arrived" anywhere. This section is what I'm watching for in my own repeat tests — not a prescription for what yours should look like.

**I'm not a doctor. I don't prescribe.** What follows is one observational lens drawn from the CIRS / biotoxin literature, framed in my own voice, with my own data referenced where I have it. Your repeat tests may not follow this pattern. Different clinicians use different stage frameworks. Some don't use stages at all. The framework doesn't tell you what to do with this information — it gives you a structured way to ask better questions about yours.

### The general trajectory I'm watching for

Based on the CIRS / biotoxin literature, transcriptomic recovery often follows a general pattern. I'm presenting it observationally because that's how I use it — to ask questions, not to plan interventions.

**Phase 1 — Innate immune is the loudest signal**

In this phase, the innate immune gene cluster (the red cluster from Section 5) is the dominant feature. Mitochondrial and coagulation genes may be near gray, or only mildly shifted.

What this might suggest: the body is mounting an active inflammatory defense. The literature connects this pattern to chronic biotoxin exposure, ongoing immune activation, and correlated serum biomarkers (C4a, TGF-beta-1, MMP-9).

What I'm watching for in my own data: are the innate immune genes clustered red? Are they correlated with elevated serum biomarkers? Is the pattern holding across repeat tests or shifting?

**Phase 2 — Coagulation pattern starting to show**

Innate immune genes still elevated, possibly stabilizing. Coagulation genes increasingly red. Mitochondrial genes may start to show mild blue.

What this might suggest: the vasculature and clotting system are responding to chronic inflammation. POTS-like symptoms, slow wound healing, brain fog that worsens with standing are the symptom patterns clinicians and patients associate with this phase in the literature.

What I'm watching for in my own data: are coagulation genes coordinated red? Does the pattern track with my orthostatic symptoms? Is PAI-1 / fibrinogen cluster tracking together?

**Phase 3 — Mitochondrial hypometabolism shows up most clearly**

Innate immune genes possibly trending back toward gray. Coagulation genes possibly stabilizing. Mitochondrial genes increasingly blue.

What this might suggest: the body's energy production has shifted toward defense mode. Fatigue, post-exertional malaise, brain fog, and reduced exercise tolerance are the symptom patterns clinicians discuss for this phase.

What I'm watching for in my own data: are multiple mitochondrial genes blue, or just one? Is the pattern correlating with my HRV (which I've been tracking daily)? Does the symptom pattern match?

**Phase 4 — Trend across repeat tests**

Multiple genes trending toward gray across repeat tests at 90-day+ intervals. The pattern over 6-12+ months matters more than any single test.

What this might suggest: the recovery signature, if it's emerging, is in the trend. The literature emphasizes that the trajectory is the signal, not any single snapshot.

What I'm watching for in my own data: are repeat tests 90+ days apart showing a coordinated shift toward gray across multiple gene clusters? Is the shift correlating with my other biomarker data — HRV, guarding score, serum C4a, environmental exposure status?

### How I use the framework

The stages aren't a protocol. They're an observational lens for asking:

- **Which gene cluster is loudest right now?** (innate immune vs coagulation vs mitochondrial)
- **Is the overall pattern improving, holding, or worsening across repeat tests?**
- **Do the GENIE findings correlate with my serum biomarkers, my symptoms, my exposure status?**
- **Where am I in the trajectory — and is the trajectory itself moving?**

The framework is observational. It helps me structure what I'm tracking. It doesn't tell me what to do about any of it. That's the conversation between me and my clinicians — and between you and yours.

### Repeat test intervals (what I'm planning)

Most CIRS / biotoxin clinicians who use GENIE in practice recommend intervals of **at least 90 days** between tests. The reasoning: shorter intervals may not show meaningful shifts in gene expression; longer intervals may miss acute changes. The trend across 3+ tests is more informative than any single test.

My plan is to repeat the GENIE panel at 90-day intervals for the next 12 months, then re-evaluate. I'll publish my repeat test patterns (with all the caveats from Section 8) as the data lands.

## Section 7 — How GENIE compares to NeuroQuant and standard serum biomarkers

The transcriptomics data from GENIE complements, but doesn't replace, two other commonly used CIRS / biotoxin assessment tools.

### Standard serum CIRS biomarkers (most common first-line testing)

**What they measure:** Protein-level markers in the blood
**Examples:** C4a, TGF-beta-1, MMP-9, VIP, MSH, ADH, osmolality, leptin, cortisol, ACTH, testosterone, DHEA, HLA-DR genetics
**What they tell you:** Current protein-level status of specific biomarkers
**What they don't tell you:** Why the levels are what they are, how they'll trend, or what the upstream gene expression pattern is

**Typical cost:** $300-1,500 depending on the panel
**Typical turnaround:** 1-3 weeks

### GENIE transcriptomics (NanoString nCounter)

**What they measure:** mRNA expression of 188 genes
**Examples:** Mitochondrial, coagulation, innate immune, apoptosis, hormonal signaling gene clusters
**What they tell you:** Which gene clusters are over- or under-expressed; pattern trajectories across repeat tests
**What they don't tell you:** Current protein levels, brain structure, clinical diagnosis

**Typical cost:** $700-1,500
**Typical turnaround:** 2-4 weeks

### NeuroQuant (volumetric MRI analysis)

**What it measures:** Volume of specific brain regions from MRI imaging
**Examples:** Hippocampal volume, caudate nucleus, putamen, ventricular volumes, cortical thickness
**What it tells you:** Structural changes in specific brain regions associated with CIRS / biotoxin illness in the research literature
**What it doesn't tell you:** Current inflammatory state, gene expression, real-time symptom pattern

**Typical cost:** $500-2,000 for the analysis (on top of MRI cost)
**Typical turnaround:** 1-2 weeks after MRI

### How they fit together

The three modalities answer different questions:
- **Serum biomarkers** = current protein-level status
- **GENIE** = current gene expression pattern + trend across repeat tests
- **NeuroQuant** = structural brain changes from prior exposure

In the PCAC framework ("show me the data, show me the before and after"), the most informative approach is to track all three modalities over time and correlate them with your symptom pattern, your environmental exposure status, and your physician's clinical assessment.

No single test tells the whole story. The brand's framework uses multiple data sources — yours and mine — to build a map, not a diagnosis.

## Section 8 — Limitations and cautions

The GENIE test is a research tool with real value, but real limitations.

### What the reference range doesn't tell you

The GENIE report's reference range is calculated from a sample of people whose blood was drawn at the lab that runs the test. The ranges don't account for:
- Your specific symptom pattern
- Your specific environmental exposure history
- Your specific genetic background
- Your specific optimal gene expression state

A "normal" gene expression level for the population may not be your personal optimal. Reference ranges are population statistics, not personal targets.

The broader transcriptomics literature acknowledges that gene expression reference ranges are sensitive to the comparator population, the RNA extraction methodology, the time of day the sample was drawn, and the patient's recent exposures, sleep, and hormonal status. A "gray" gene on a GENIE report is not a fixed health claim — it's a measurement relative to a chosen reference.

**Reference:** Mohr S, Liew CC. The peripheral-blood transcriptome: new insights into disease and risk assessment. *Trends in Molecular Medicine.* 2007;13(10):422-432. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.molmed.2007.08.003

### What single-timepoint snapshots miss

A single GENIE test is a snapshot. The gene expression pattern can shift with:
- Acute environmental exposure events
- Acute infections
- Medications and supplements
- Hormonal cycles
- Sleep quality
- Exercise
- Diet

A single test in isolation is hard to interpret. The trend across 3+ tests over 6-12+ months is more informative.

### What transcriptomics can't directly measure

mRNA is an upstream signal. It tells you how actively a gene is being transcribed. It doesn't directly tell you:
- The protein level in the cell (mRNA → protein translation can be regulated independently)
- The functional activity of the protein (post-translational modifications matter)
- The cellular context (tissue-specific expression, cell-type-specific signaling)

The GENIE panel is a signal about what the cell is doing at the gene transcription level. It is one input among many for understanding your overall state.

The central dogma caveat is well-established in molecular biology: transcription (DNA → mRNA) and translation (mRNA → protein) are independently regulated. A transcriptomic signal is one layer of the system; proteomic and metabolomic data (lactate, pyruvate, organic acids, plasma amino acids) tell the downstream functional story. The brand's framework tracks multiple layers — GENIE for the upstream transcriptomic signal, serum biomarkers for protein-level CIRS activity, daily HRV for autonomic nervous system function, TyTron for autonomic nerve interference — to build a multi-modal map.

**Reference:** Schwanhäusser B, et al. Global quantification of mammalian gene expression control. *Nature.* 2011;473:337-342. https://doi.org/10.1038/nature10098

### What this guide doesn't replace

This guide is an interpretation decoder, not a clinical recommendation. It does not replace:
- Working with a qualified CIRS / biotoxin clinician
- Standard clinical workup (history, physical exam, differential diagnosis)
- Environmental testing (ERMI, HERTSMI-2, Actinos panels)
- Standard serum CIRS biomarkers
