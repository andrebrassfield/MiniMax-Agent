**Tweet 2:**

Background.

KPV is a short tripeptide fragment of alpha-melanocyte-stimulating hormone (α-MSH). It has been studied for anti-inflammatory effects, primarily in murine models of inflammatory bowel disease.

The mechanism is well-characterized in animal literature. Human data is limited.

---

**Tweet 3:**

Regulatory state going in:

Like BPC-157, KPV was on the Category 2 bulk substances list and was removed in April 2026.

The Category 2 placement cited "significant safety risks" + insufficient characterization for human use — manufacturing and safety concerns, not efficacy findings.

---

**Tweet 4:**

What's actually being evaluated July 23:

The PCAC will review available data on KPV for wound healing and inflammatory conditions and vote on whether to recommend inclusion on the 503A Bulks List.

This is a procedural evaluation, not an approval hearing.

---

**Tweet 5:**

The non-binding nature matters here.

A PCAC "yes" is a recommendation. The FDA weighs it, may accept or reject it, and issues its own determination on 503A Bulks List inclusion.

If the FDA follows a positive recommendation, state-licensed 503A pharmacies could compound KPV under physician prescription. Nothing more.

---

**Tweet 6:**

What this would and would not change:

Would change: legal pathway for physician-supervised access via compounding pharmacies.

Would NOT change: FDA-approval status (still unapproved for any indication), direct-to-consumer legality (still illegal), gray-market sourcing (still illegal), and "research only" labeling that names human therapeutic uses (still misbranding under 21 CFR 201.128).

---

**Tweet 7:**

"My body, not yours" close:

Inflammatory conditions are real and serious. The mechanisms KPV touches are well-described in the literature. None of that changes the legal architecture around access.

Physician-supervised, compounded, prescribed. That sequence exists for a reason. Skipping any step puts the entire operation in the FDA's enforcement lane.
