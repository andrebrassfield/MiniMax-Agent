---

**Tweet 2:**

Disclaimers live on the page you wrote.

The FDA reads: alt text on your product images. URL slugs on your blog posts. Schema markup behind the scenes. Meta descriptions that auto-populate when someone shares your link.

The "research only" copy on your homepage is one input. The backend is the rest.

---

**Tweet 3:**

Concrete examples the agency treats as evidence of intended use:

— Bidding on or ranking for "BPC-157 for gut healing"
— URL slug: /peptides-for-anxiety/
— Alt text on a vial image: "BPC-157 injection for connective tissue repair"
— Meta description: "Compounded BPC-157 for tendon recovery."

These are the signals regulators crawl first.

---

**Tweet 4:**

Schema is the easy miss.

If your product page emits `Product` schema with claims like "treatment for X," "effective for Y," or therapeutic keywords in the description field — search engines and regulators both see that.

The JSON-LD sits in your source. It's not a hidden field. It's public.

---

**Tweet 5:**

This is why "we never claimed it was for humans" stops working the moment your SEO or schema does.

The Objective Intent Doctrine (21 CFR 201.128) weighs the whole operation. The FDA doesn't accept "our developer made a typo" as a defense for a /peptides-for-anxiety slug.

---

**Tweet 6:**

Audit yourself before someone else does.

Three checks you can run today:
1. Pull every image alt text on product pages.
2. Grep your URL slugs for therapeutic keywords.
3. View source on product pages and read the JSON-LD.

Anything that reads as a clinical claim is a future warning letter.

"My body, not yours" close:

The reason I built this brand on proof over claims is exactly this. The data layer — biomarkers, scans, my own symptom log — survives any audit.

What I track in my own body is mine to publish. What I claim a compound does in yours is a federal case waiting to happen.
