**Tweet 2:**

Section 505(a) defines a misbranded drug in part by its labeling.

"Labeling" isn't just the bottle. It's every statement that accompanies the product — website copy, ads, AND content you host or feature.

If a comment claims "[compound] healed my [condition]" and you leave it up, the FDA can treat that as your labeling.

---

**Tweet 3:**

This applies even when:
— the comment is in a private community you moderate
— the testimonial is on a third-party review site you link to
— the user wrote it without you asking
— you put a "results not typical" disclaimer above the comments section

Hosting adopts.

---

**Tweet 4:**

The practical rule:

Delete. Or heavily qualify.

"Delete" means remove the comment, screenshot it for your records, and document why you deleted it.

"Heavily qualify" means visibly mark it as third-party experience, not your product's claim — and even then, the FDA can still push back.

---

**Tweet 5:**

There is no middle ground of "well they wrote it, not me."

The FDA's framing: you built the venue. You curate the venue. You profit from the venue. The user-generated content in your venue is part of your labeling.

The legal exposure flows to you.

---

**Tweet 6:**

This is why I rebuilt the Dose of Proof site last week.

Every "verified member" testimonial that named a specific compound or claimed a specific outcome got deleted or rewritten. The product data that mentioned "sourcing links" or "exact dosing protocols" got reframed to research education only.

The model has to match the message.

---

**Tweet 7:**

"My body, not yours" close:

This is the rule that keeps me clean: every story I tell is mine.

My HRV. My TyTron scans. My flushing episodes. My own data, from my own body, in my own words.

The moment you start extrapolating other people's outcomes to a compound, you are no longer telling a story. You are making a claim.
