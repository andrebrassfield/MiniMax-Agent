**Tweet 2:**

The FDA does not need your marketing copy to convict you.

Under 21 CFR 201.128, "intended use" is established by the objective characteristics of the product itself — not by the disclaimer you wrote at the bottom of the page.

A "research only" label is one data point. The FDA weighs all of them.

---

**Tweet 3:**

Specific example.

Listing BPC-157 or TB-500 on the same product page or cart as:
— bacteriostatic water
— insulin syringes
— 29–31 gauge injection supplies

…is treated as evidence of intended subcutaneous delivery to humans.

The "research only" sticker doesn't override what the cart shows.

---

**Tweet 4:**

This isn't theoretical.

In March 2023 the FDA issued a Warning Letter to Grand Health Inc. (doing business as "Gram Peptides") for selling tirzepatide and retatrutide alongside bacteriostatic water and related supplies.

The agency cited the integrated offering as proof of intended human use.

---

**Tweet 5:**

The Warning Letter is public. Anyone can read it.

What the FDA actually wrote: when the product is sold as part of a kit that includes reconstitution supplies, the whole offering is treated as an unapproved new drug — not as a research reagent.

Disclaimers were present on the site. They did not protect the company.

---

**Tweet 6:**

This is the trap.

Most creators think: "I added 'not for human use' — I'm covered."

The FDA reads: kitting + supplies + therapeutic keywords + pricing in human-relevant units = intended use.

The label is one input. The whole operation is the evidence.
