## Title

**How to Read Your Own Bloodwork — What 4 Specialists Never Explained**

*Reference ranges are population statistics, not your personal targets. Here's the difference.*

## Subtitle

A practical guide to reading your labs for *your* data — not the population's — written from the inside of 18 months of self-tracking and 4 specialists who never connected the dots.

## Body

I've pulled my own bloodwork 6 times in the last 18 months.

The first 4 times, I didn't know what I was looking at. The doctor would say "your labs are normal," hand me the printout, and I'd leave. I'd stare at the numbers on the drive home without context. Most of the test names were alphabet soup — TSH, ESR, RBC, MCV. The reference ranges at the right edge of each column looked like a foreign language.

The 5th time, I caught the trend before my doctor did. A subtle pattern in my Vitamin D — borderline low, then low, then lower — across three draws. I came in with the data. She ordered the test again and adjusted the protocol.

The 6th time, I built the spreadsheet my physicians now ask me to share.

This post is how I learned to read my own labs — and what 4 specialists never explained to me.

---

## Why reference ranges miss most people

Most "how to read your bloodwork" guides tell you what the reference ranges mean. That's the wrong starting point.

Reference ranges are population statistics. They're calculated from a sample of people whose blood was drawn at the lab that runs the test. The ranges don't account for your specific symptoms, your specific terrain, or your specific optimal state.

A "normal" Vitamin D of 32 ng/mL means 95% of the lab's reference population falls between 30 and 100. It does NOT mean 32 is optimal for you — it means 32 is common.

If your symptoms suggest a Vitamin D issue, and your level is 32, that's a data point, not a verdict.

The reference range is the population's range. Your optimal range is wherever your specific symptoms resolve.

---

## The 3 shifts that changed how I read my labs

**Shift 1 — From single values to trends.**

One Vitamin D reading of 28 is a snapshot. Three Vitamin D readings of 32, 28, 24 over 12 months is a trend. Trends matter more than snapshots.

When I started tracking the same markers quarterly, I started seeing patterns no single draw could show. My Vitamin D was declining despite supplementation. My HRV correlated with sleep quality. My fasting glucose correlated with carb load the prior week.

The trends are where the signal lives.

**Shift 2 — From reference range to personal optimal.**

Once you have 3-4 data points for a marker, you can ask: at what value did my symptoms resolve?

My personal optimal Vitamin D turned out to be 50-60 ng/mL — within the upper half of the reference range, not the lower. My personal optimal fasting glucose is 78-85 mg/dL — within range, but tighter than the population band suggests.

Personal optimal is empirical. Reference range is statistical. They often overlap but they're not the same thing.

**Shift 3 — From "lab normal" to "pattern normal."**

A single "normal" reading of every marker doesn't mean you're healthy. It means each marker fell within the population range.

Pattern recognition matters more: how do the markers relate to each other? Is your high-sensitivity CRP low while your ESR is high? That suggests the inflammation is upstream of the markers — likely in the tissue, not the blood. Is your Vitamin D borderline while your PTH is at the top of the range? That suggests your body is using stored Vitamin D aggressively, not that you're fine.

Patterns are upstream of values.

## The 5-marker floor (revisited with reading lens)

Here's how I read each of the 5 biomarkers in my own framework — the floor that catches the upstream drivers most doctors miss.

**1. Morning HRV (Heart Rate Variability)**

Reference range: most apps report 0-100, but the meaningful range is 20-80 for adults.

What I look for:
- 7-day running average (not single days — single days are noisy)
- The standard deviation around the average (variability)
- Trend over 30, 60, 90 days

My personal optimal: 7-day average 50-60 with SD under 8.

When my HRV drops below 45 average for 5+ days, something upstream is flaring — usually the upper neck or sleep. I don't wait for a single bad day; I watch the 5-day average.

**2. TyTron Paraspinal Thermal Pattern**

This isn't a single number. It's a pattern — left vs right asymmetry along the paraspinal muscles, plus the "waviness" of the temperature line.

What I look for:
- Symmetry between left and right at each vertebral level
- Straightness of the lines (wavy = nerve interference)
- Change after an adjustment (immediate visual feedback)

The TyTron doesn't give a "value" in the traditional lab sense. It gives a visual pattern. The reading is qualitative, but it's also the most direct feedback on whether the mechanical driver is being addressed.

**3. Tryptase + Urine MCAS Mediators**

Reference range: tryptase typically <10 ng/mL; urine mediators have their own reference ranges per lab.

What I look for:
- Whether baseline tryptase is meaningful (often it isn't in MCAS — baseline can be normal)
- Whether urine mediators caught during/after a flare show elevation
- The pattern: N-methylhistamine + LTE4 + prostaglandin D2 metabolites all elevated = mast cell degranulation event

The reading lens: don't trust a single baseline tryptase in isolation. Catch the flare. The data that matters is the flare data, not the calm-day data.

**4. ESR + CRP + Symptom Tracking**

Reference range: ESR <20 mm/hr for men, CRP <3 mg/L (high-sensitivity CRP <1 mg/L for low-inflammation baseline).

What I look for:
- Whether CRP and ESR agree (both up = systemic inflammation; one up, one normal = localized)
- Whether my subjective guarding score (1-10) tracks with the blood markers
- Whether flushing episodes correlate with CRP spikes

The reading lens: subjective symptoms + objective markers together = the upstream-to-downstream story. Either alone is incomplete.

**5. Vitamin D + Magnesium Status**

Reference range: 25-OH Vitamin D typically 30-100 ng/mL; RBC magnesium 4.2-6.8 mg/dL; serum magnesium 1.7-2.4 mg/dL.

What I look for:
- Vitamin D trend over quarters (not single draws)
- Magnesium status via RBC (more accurate than serum)
- Whether low Vitamin D correlates with high PTH (parathyroid hormone compensating)

The reading lens: low Vitamin D + high PTH = your body is using Vitamin D faster than you're replacing it. The standard "take 2000 IU daily" advice may not be enough for your terrain.

## What to do with this when you bring it to your physician

Bring your spreadsheet, not just your printout. Most physicians will engage with a trend table more readily than a single lab value.

Frame the conversation around patterns, not single numbers:

> "My Vitamin D has been 32, 28, 24 over the last year. The reference range is 30-100 but the trend is declining. Should we look at why it's declining rather than just supplementing?"

> "My fasting glucose is 92 — within range — but my HbA1c is 5.6 — at the top of normal. Both readings suggest something is changing. Should we look at the pattern?"

Most physicians will engage with this framing. The few who won't — and you may have one — are signaling they don't work well with informed patients. That's data too.
