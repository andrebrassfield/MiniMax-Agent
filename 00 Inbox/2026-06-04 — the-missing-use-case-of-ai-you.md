## The article (verbatim)

> The Missing Use Case of AI: You.
>
> @mphrediction, Jun 2
>
> Most conversations about AI are still productivity focused. AI will write emails, make decks, replace admin, automate jobs, make workers faster, cheaper, and more efficient. That is all true, however, I believe it misses the bigger picture.
>
> i. Introduction
> The reason I am writing this is because this subject has been a huge interest of mine for quite some time. I have always been interested in people: how they think, what they hide, what they struggle with, and why so many people can look "fine" on the outside while quietly breaking down internally. I believe a lot of people are "bad" people because of this and really, containing emotion on the inside is like a coke bottle as mentos gum hits.
>
> You bottling up your emotions
>
> And now, more than ever, I think we are living in a world where people are surrounded by communication but starved of connection.
> We have phones in our hands all day. We can message anyone instantly. We can watch people's lives in real time. But somehow, a lot of people feel more alone, anxious, and less able to say what they really feel.
>
> That is where AI becomes interesting in my eyes and I believe there is a market about to be formed where an AI is a companion, rather than just a useful tool you use from time to time.
>
> The missing use case of AI is not work. It is you and your well-being.
>
> ii. AI usage is rising while real-world socializing is falling
>
> AI chatbots are already becoming normal, especially among young people. A 2026 RAND study found that 19.2% of U.S. adolescents and young adults aged 12 to 21 had used AI chatbots such as ChatGPT, Gemini, or Meta AI for help when feeling sad, angry, nervous, or stressed. That was up from 13.1% in a similar survey the year before. RAND estimated this represented around 8.2 million young people in the U.S. alone. (rand.org)
>
> At the same time, young people are spending less time socializing in person. A major study by Twenge, Spitzberg, and Campbell found that adolescents in the 2010s spent less time in face-to-face interaction than previous generations. College-bound high school seniors in 2016 spent about one hour less per day in in-person social interaction than those in the late 1980s, while adolescent loneliness rose sharply after 2011. (Sage Journals)
>
> Phones also affect the quality of real-world interaction. Dwyer, Kushlev, and Dunn found that when phones were present during meals with friends or family, people felt more distracted and enjoyed the interaction less. (ScienceDirect)
>
> This is the link people are missing. As the world becomes more digitally connected, it can also become less emotionally open. People are not necessarily sharing less because they have nothing to say. They may be sharing less because the spaces where real vulnerability used to happen are disappearing.
>
> So when an AI chatbot is available 24/7, never embarrassed, never busy, never judgemental, and always ready to respond, it starts to fill a gap. Not because it is better than people. Because, for many people, it is easier than people.
>
> iii. Companionship will become AI's biggest consumer use case
>
> The strongest sign of this shift is that people are already using AI for emotional support.

### Section iv — From AI therapists to AI friends, companions, and robots (verbatim)

> The next stage is obvious: AI will not only answer questions. It will become more personable.

> Robots in the home could begin as assistants: helping elderly people, reminding people to take medication, helping with chores, keeping children entertained, or supporting people with disabilities.
>
> But once those robots have advanced conversational AI inside them, the relationship changes.
>
> A home robot that knows your routines, notices your mood, plays your favourite music, encourages you when you are low, and remembers what you said last week is not just a machine in the corner. It becomes part of the emotional environment of the home.
>
> For some people, these robots may become companions. For others, carers. For others, friends. And for some, possibly something even more intimate.
>
> That sounds futuristic, but the emotional foundation is already being laid through chatbots. The screen comes first. The voice comes next. Then the body. The possibilities are endless, but so are the risks.
>
> A world where AI helps lonely people feel heard could be a good thing. But a world where people choose AI because human relationships feel too difficult, too expensive, or too unsafe is something we need to take seriously.
